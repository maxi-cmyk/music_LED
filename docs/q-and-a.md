# Fourier demo Q&A bank

1. **What sign and normalization do you use?** The forward exponent is negative and the forward transform is unnormalized. The inverse therefore includes $1/N$.
2. **Is the entire sound-to-colour system linear?** No. The DFT is linear, but magnitude, thresholding, clipping, and RGB mapping introduce nonlinear steps.
3. **How did you verify correctness?** Known inputs have expected coefficients, DFT and FFT full complex outputs agree at four sizes, and a numerical superposition test verifies linearity.
4. **Why is the live frame 128 samples?** It is a power of two for radix-2 FFT and gives a short 20 ms nominal frame at 6400 samples per second. The bins are then $f_s/N=6400/128=50\,\mathrm{Hz}$ apart.
5. **Does 50 Hz bin spacing mean any two tones 50 Hz apart are always resolved?** No. Resolution also depends on frame length, windowing, signal strength, noise, and leakage.
6. **Does the project have a verified anti-alias filter?** No verified external filter is claimed, so controlled tones stay below Nyquist and music results are qualitative.
7. **Why apply a Hamming window?** It reduces leakage from discontinuities at the frame edges, while broadening peaks and changing magnitude scaling.
8. **What happens at 225 Hz?** It lies between 200 and 250 Hz bin centres, so its energy spreads across nearby coefficients rather than occupying one bin.
9. **Does the timing table prove asymptotic complexity?** No. It measures these implementations at finite sizes and supports the structural explanation.
10. **What exactly is timed?** Only the transform call. Acquisition, preprocessing, output magnitude, serial printing, LED updates, and test-signal construction are outside the timed region. The current FFT call includes its internal copy and twiddle calculations.
11. **How is measurement noise handled?** Runs are warmed up and repeated; the median, minimum, and maximum are reported, and a checksum consumes outputs.
12. **How are frequencies mapped to colour?** Bins 1–5 contribute red, 6–20 green, and 21–50 blue. DC and higher bins do not contribute.
13. **Why use fixed gains?** Fixed calibration preserves comparisons. Independent per-band normalization could make a weak component look falsely equal to a strong one.
14. **Why might equal generated tones not look yellow?** The speaker, room, microphone, electronics, and LED channels have different responses, so physical calibration is required.
15. **Why are only rows 0–64 used?** For real input, row $128-k$ is the complex conjugate of row $k$: $F_{128-k,n}=e^{-i2\pi n}\,e^{+i2\pi kn/128}=\overline{F_{k,n}}$, because $e^{-i2\pi n}=1$ for whole-number $n$. So $X_{128-k}=\overline{X_{k}}$ and $|X_{128-k}|=|X_{k}|$. Rows 65–127 repeat rows 63–1 and add no information. Rows 0 (DC) and 64 (Nyquist, 3200 Hz) are their own partners, which leaves $1+63+1=65$ distinct frequencies.
16. **Why is anything mirrored at all?** A real sine is two rotations, $\cos\theta=\tfrac12(e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta})$, so a 200 Hz tone has energy at $+200$ Hz and $-200$ Hz. At 6400 samples per second, $-200$ Hz produces exactly the same samples as $6400-200=6200$ Hz, which is row 124. Nothing is mirrored by choice; it follows from the input being real.
17. **Why does row 0 not drive a colour?** Row 0 is the frame's average level, not sound. The microphone sits at a DC bias near mid-scale, and the firmware subtracts the frame mean before the FFT anyway. Counting row 0 as bass would make the LED react to bias rather than music.
18. **Why does the LED stop at row 50 (2500 Hz) instead of row 64 (3200 Hz)?** To keep a margin below the 3200 Hz Nyquist limit. The ESP32 ADC has no anti-alias filter, so sound above 3200 Hz folds back into the top rows (a 3500 Hz sound appears at $6400-3500=2900$ Hz, row 58), and the Hamming window spreads some energy into neighbouring rows. Stopping at row 50 (`TREBLE_LAST_BIN` in `LightingConfig.h`) keeps that folded and spilled energy out of the LED.
19. **What does the leakage step do?** It finds the strongest weighted channel, sets a floor $t=0.15\max(\mathbf{u})$, subtracts $t$ from every channel and clamps at zero. A channel under 15% of the dominant one is treated as spill from that band and switched off. Dividing by $0.85$ returns the strongest channel to its original value, so only weak channels change.
20. **Where does 0.12 come from?** `BRIGHTNESS_PER_MAGNITUDE_UNIT = 0.12` is the common scale that turns a band's strength into LED brightness (0–255). We set it by testing: single test tones through our speaker and microphone gave band strengths of about 2,200–2,600, and 0.12 makes those tones reach full brightness ($255/0.12\approx2125$, just below the weakest). It was tuned for our speaker, room and volume, so it must be retuned if any of them change. As a rough guide, with the Hamming window a pure tone of amplitude $A$ ADC counts gives $|X_{k}|\approx 0.54\cdot\tfrac{128}{2}A\approx 34.6A$, and the two neighbouring rows add about $14.7A$ each, so the band strength is about $40A$. Full brightness therefore corresponds to a tone of roughly 50–60 ADC counts, under 3% of the 12-bit range, before the channel gains are applied.
21. **Why must $F$ be square? Could it be tall?** A tall $M\times N$ matrix ($M>N$) with full column rank is still injective, so $\mathbf{x}$ can be recovered with a left inverse and no information is lost. It has no two-sided inverse, though, and its $M$ outputs are redundant: they are coordinates in an overcomplete set (a frame), not in a basis. We use a square $N\times N$ matrix so $N$ samples map to exactly $N$ coefficients, a genuine change of basis. Tall versions exist in practice: a zero-padded (oversampled) DFT samples the spectrum more finely without adding information.

22. **Why is $F_N$ dense but its factors sparse?** $F_N$ has no zero entries, but its four blocks are built from the same half-size matrix $F_{N/2}$ (after the even columns are moved first): the top-left and bottom-left blocks are both $F_{N/2}$, and the right blocks are $DF_{N/2}$ and $-DF_{N/2}$. Pulling the repeated $F_{N/2}$ out leaves $B_N=\begin{bmatrix}I&D\\I&-D\end{bmatrix}$, which holds only identity and diagonal blocks, so each row has two non-zero entries. Repeating on $F_{N/2}$ gives $F_N=A_m\cdots A_2A_1P$.
23. **Is the FFT an approximation of the DFT?** No. It is an exact factorisation of the same matrix, $F_N=A_m\cdots A_1P$, so the two routes differ only by floating-point rounding. Checked on the ESP32's own frame (128 samples), the largest difference between the two spectra is below $10^{-14}$ in 64-bit floats. The firmware itself uses 32-bit `float`, so on the device the two routes agree only to ordinary single-precision rounding, not to $10^{-14}$.
24. **How many butterflies and how many non-zero entries?** Each stage has $N/2=64$ butterflies, and there are $\log_2 128=7$ stages, so $448$ butterflies. A butterfly uses 4 non-zero entries (two 1s and $\pm W$), so $448$ butterflies hold $1792$ non-zero entries, which is $7$ factors of $256$, against $128^2=16384$ in $F_{128}$.
25. **Why does one split save about half the dense work?** A dense $N\times N$ matrix costs $N^2$ products. After the split there are two dense $\tfrac N2\times\tfrac N2$ matrices, $2(\tfrac N2)^2=N^2/2$, plus the sparse $B_N$ with $2N$ non-zero entries. Repeating this $\log_2N$ times gives about $2N\log_2N$ in total.
26. **Is the permutation $P$ built as a matrix in the code?** No. $P$ has a single 1 in each row and column, with row $i$ having its 1 in column $\mathrm{rev}(i)$, the index with its bits reversed. Multiplying by it only reorders the samples, so the firmware swaps $x_i$ with $x_{\mathrm{rev}(i)}$ whenever $i<\mathrm{rev}(i)$. For $N=128$ that is 56 swaps.
27. **Why is the reordering a bit reversal?** Each even/odd split reads one bit of the index: bit $b_0$ decides which half, $b_1$ which quarter, and so on. After all the splits the first bit read becomes the top bit of the new position, so position $=\mathrm{rev}(n)=(b_0b_1\cdots b_{m-1})_2$.
28. **How does the C++ map onto the matrices?** `compute_fast_fourier_transform` copies the samples into the spectrum arrays (imaginary parts 0) and makes two calls. `reorder_bit_reversed_samples` is $\boldsymbol{P}$. `apply_fft_stages` makes $\log_2N$ passes with block length $L=2,4,\dots,N$, and each pass is one matrix $\boldsymbol{A}_s$. Inside a pass, `combine_subtransforms` runs the butterflies of one block, which is one block $\boldsymbol{B}_L$ of $\boldsymbol{A}_s$.
29. **Do these counts say how fast the ESP32 runs it?** No. They are operation counts. A butterfly and a product are different amounts of work, and real time also includes memory access and the loop overhead; the timing measurements (Q9–Q11) are separate.
30. **Why does $T(N)=2T(N/2)+O(N)$ give $O(N\log N)$?** Each level of the recursion has pieces whose sizes add up to $N$, so each level costs $O(N)$ (for the FFT, $N/2$ butterflies). Halving $N$ each time gives $\log_2N$ levels, so the total is $O(N)$ per level times $\log_2N$ levels, which is $O(N\log N)$. For $N=128$ that is 7 levels of 64 butterflies, 448 in all.

31. **Is bit shifting what makes the FFT fast?** No. The saving from $O(N^2)$ to $O(N\log N)$ comes from reusing the half-size blocks. Bit operations make the bookkeeping cheap: one even/odd split is a rotate right of the index bits, and the full reordering is the permutation matrix $P$. As a dense matrix product $P\mathbf{x}$ would cost $N^2=16384$ multiply-adds for $N=128$; the firmware does the same job with 56 swaps and no multiplications, using `>>`, `&` and `^`.
32. **Why must $N$ be a power of two?** The split halves the problem evenly down to single samples, so $N=2^m$ and every index is an $m$-bit number: each split reads one bit. The firmware checks this with `n & (n - 1) == 0` and rejects other sizes. Other lengths need a mixed-radix FFT or zero-padding to the next power of two.
33. **How is $\Pi_N$ related to the index bits?** $\Pi_N$ puts the even samples first, then the odd samples. The sample at index $n=(b_{m-1}\cdots b_1b_0)_2$ moves to position $(b_0b_{m-1}\cdots b_1)_2$, a rotate right of the index by one bit. The next level rotates the lower $m-1$ bits inside each half, and the product of all the levels is the bit-reversal matrix $P$, with $P_{ij}=1$ exactly when $j=\mathrm{rev}(i)$.
34. **How many row exchanges does $P$ need?** The rows that stay put are the indices whose bit string reads the same backwards, $2^{\lceil m/2\rceil}$ of them, and the rest swap in pairs: $(N-2^{\lceil m/2\rceil})/2$ exchanges. For $N=128$ that is $(128-16)/2=56$.
35. **Is the FFT done in place?** Yes. A butterfly reads the two entries $u$ and $v$ and writes $u+W_L^{j}v$ and $u-W_L^{j}v$ back over the same two positions, and the reorder is a set of swaps. So one pair of arrays (real and imaginary) holds the input, every intermediate stage and the result. No second copy of the spectrum is needed.
36. **How does the firmware get the twiddle factors $W_L^{j}$?** Once per pass it calls `cosf` and `sinf` to get the step $W_L$. Inside a block it starts the twiddle at $1$ and multiplies it by the step after every butterfly (the line `twiddle *= step` in the shortened listing), so it never calls sine or cosine per butterfly. The trade-off is that rounding error builds up along a block, which stays small because a block has at most $N/2=64$ steps. A precomputed table is the usual alternative.
37. **Why do the passes go from small blocks to large ones when the formula reads $\boldsymbol{A}_m\cdots\boldsymbol{A}_1\boldsymbol{P}$?** A matrix product acts on the vector from the right. So $\boldsymbol{P}$ acts first, then $\boldsymbol{A}_1$ (block length $L=2$), and $\boldsymbol{A}_m$ (block length $L=N$) acts last. The loop `for (L = 2; L <= N; L <<= 1)` is the product read right to left. Each $\boldsymbol{A}_s$ is block diagonal, with $N/L$ copies of $\boldsymbol{B}_L$ on the diagonal, which is why the inner loop can handle each block separately.
38. **How many complex operations does the firmware do for $N=128$, against the direct DFT?** The direct DFT needs $N^2=16384$ complex products. The FFT code does $448$ butterflies, each with one complex product by the twiddle and one more to update the twiddle, so $896$ complex products, plus $2$ complex additions per butterfly. The $O(N\log N)$ against $O(N^2)$ gap is the point; these are operation counts, not timings (see Q29).
39. **What are the limits of the measurement chain?** The microphone is an analog module (KY-037), tuned by hand with its sensitivity knob rather than against a sound-level reference, and read by the ESP32's 12-bit ADC. That ADC is nonlinear and noisy, it is ADC2 on GPIO 4 so Wi-Fi has to stay off, and its readings are counts, not sound pressure, so magnitudes are relative. There is no verified anti-alias filter, so energy above the 3200 Hz Nyquist limit folds into lower bins. The 6400 Hz rate is software-timed, so every frame is sampled with a little jitter, which the firmware measures but does not remove.
40. **What limits the frequency and time resolution?** Bins are $f_s/N=50\,\mathrm{Hz}$ apart, so two tones closer than that can blur together, and the Hamming window broadens each peak further. A tone between bin centres, such as 225 Hz, spreads across neighbouring bins. A frame covers about 20 ms and frames start at least 30 ms apart, so some of the sound is never analysed, and the LED lags the sound by at least the 20 ms sampling time plus the computing time. A frame is treated as a steady sound, so changes inside it are blurred.
41. **What does the LED not show?** Three brightness values summarise bins 1–50. Phase is discarded, the DC bin and the bins above 50 are ignored, and the original sound cannot be rebuilt. The band edges, the 15% leakage cut and the 0.12 brightness scale are hand-tuned design choices, and the fixed channel gains were set empirically after testing with our speaker, room and volume, so the colour balance holds for that setup and has to be redone if it changes. After the DFT the pipeline is nonlinear (magnitudes, thresholding, clipping), so a good-looking LED shows that the mapping works, not that the transform is correct.
42. **What are the limits of the algorithm and the code?** The radix-2 FFT needs $N$ to be a power of two, and the firmware rejects other sizes. It runs a full complex FFT on real input, so about half of its work is redundant. It uses 32-bit `float`, whose rounding error is far smaller than the quantisation of the 12-bit ADC but much larger than a 64-bit reference.
43. **What has and has not been verified?** The DFT and FFT are tested against known signals and against each other, coefficient by coefficient, and those checks and the benchmark run on a development computer. The timing table is from that computer, not the ESP32, and operation counts are not timings, so no ESP32 speedup is claimed. Results with live music are qualitative: the microphone knob and the colour gains were tuned by testing, but there is no calibrated measurement of the microphone or LED response.
44. **Why did you use radix-2 specifically?** Mostly because it is the easiest to write and to check when $N$ is a power of two, and our frame is $N=128=2^7$. Every split halves the problem exactly, every index is a 7-bit number, the only operation is the butterfly, and the reordering is a bit reversal that can be done in place. It also matches the factorisation in the slides, $\boldsymbol{F}_N=\boldsymbol{A}_m\cdots\boldsymbol{A}_1\boldsymbol{P}$, where each $\boldsymbol{A}_s$ is block diagonal with sparse blocks $\boldsymbol{B}_L$ made of identity and diagonal pieces, so the code can be read against the algebra. It is not the cheapest choice: radix-4 needs about 25% fewer complex multiplications and split-radix needs fewer operations still. We chose readability over those savings.
45. **Why not radix-4?** Radix-4 combines four sub-transforms at once, which cuts the complex multiplications from $\tfrac N2\log_2N$ to about $\tfrac38N\log_2N$, roughly 25% fewer. It needs $N$ to be a power of 4, and 128 is not (256 is). For 128 you would use three radix-4 stages plus one radix-2 stage, which is more code and more cases to check.
46. **Why is the sampling rate only 6400 Hz?** It was our choice for a simple demo, and it is enough: the LED only uses the 50–2,500 Hz part of the spectrum (bins 1–50), so a higher sample rate would add no new frequencies for it to use. Speed is not the reason; the ESP32 could handle more. A higher rate also isn't free with a 128-sample window. At 44.1 kHz, 128 samples last only 3 ms and each frequency slot becomes 344 Hz wide, so all the bass would land in one slot. Keeping 50 Hz slots would need 1,024 samples per frame, about 11 times more work, which is doable. The downside is that we have no anti-alias filter in front of the ADC, so folding becomes an issue for sound beyond the Nyquist frequency. That is also why we stop at 2,500 Hz instead of 3,200 Hz.
47. **Why is $\boldsymbol{F}$ invertible?** $\boldsymbol{F}$ is a square matrix: 128 rows (one test wave per pitch) and 128 columns (one per sample). Its rows are orthogonal: each test wave has nothing in common with any other, so $\boldsymbol{F}$ multiplied by its conjugate gives 128 times the identity matrix. Orthogonal, non-zero rows are linearly independent: no row can be built by mixing the others. 128 linearly independent rows means full rank (rank 128). A square matrix with full rank is invertible, so we can undo the transform and rebuild the 128 samples from the 128 answers. Both steps matter. Being different is not enough: $[1,1]$ and $[2,2]$ are different, but the second is just twice the first, so they are not independent. And being full rank is not enough without being square: a tall matrix can have full rank and still have no inverse.
48. **What is the conjugate transpose, and why do we need it?** Two small steps. First transpose: flip the matrix over its main diagonal, so row 1 becomes column 1. Then conjugate: turn every $i$ into $-i$. For example, $\begin{bmatrix}1&2i\\3&4-i\end{bmatrix}$ becomes $\begin{bmatrix}1&3\\2i&4-i\end{bmatrix}$ after transposing, and $\begin{bmatrix}1&3\\-2i&4+i\end{bmatrix}$ after conjugating. That final matrix is the conjugate transpose, written with a small $H$. Why we need it: take the vector $(1,i)$. The usual dot product of it with itself is 1 times 1 plus $i$ times $i$, which equals $0$, as if a non-zero vector had no length. If we conjugate the first copy we get 1 times 1 plus $-i$ times $i$, which equals $2$, a proper length. So for complex vectors the correct dot product uses the conjugate, and when it is 0 the two vectors are orthogonal. This is what we check on $\boldsymbol{F}$: $\boldsymbol{F}$ times its conjugate transpose is 128 times the identity, so every row of $\boldsymbol{F}$ is orthogonal to every other row. For our $\boldsymbol{F}$ the transpose changes nothing ($\boldsymbol{F}$ is symmetric), so its conjugate transpose is just $\boldsymbol{F}$ with every $i$ turned into $-i$: the same waves spinning the other way. Dividing by 128 gives the inverse transform.
49. **Where does the butterfly matrix $\boldsymbol{B}_N$ come from?** The FFT builds the full answer from two half-size answers: $\mathbf{E}$, from the even-numbered samples, and $\mathbf{O}$, from the odd-numbered ones. The rule is that the first half of the full answer is $\mathbf{E}+\boldsymbol{D}\mathbf{O}$, and the second half is $\mathbf{E}-\boldsymbol{D}\mathbf{O}$, where $\boldsymbol{D}$ is a diagonal matrix of rotation factors (the twiddle factors). Stack $\mathbf{E}$ on top of $\mathbf{O}$ as one vector, and that rule is exactly $\boldsymbol{B}_N=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{I}&\boldsymbol{D}\\\boldsymbol{I}&-\boldsymbol{D}\end{bmatrix}$: $\boldsymbol{I}$ passes $\mathbf{E}$ through, $\boldsymbol{D}$ rotates $\mathbf{O}$, and the minus sign in the bottom row gives $\mathbf{E}-\boldsymbol{D}\mathbf{O}$. Each row has only two non-zero entries, so applying it is cheap. One FFT stage is this matrix repeated for every block, and it is the matrix $\boldsymbol{B}_N$ in the slides' factorisation of $\boldsymbol{F}_N$.
50. **What is the inverse of $\boldsymbol{F}$, and what does it show?** $\boldsymbol{F}^{-1}=\tfrac1N\boldsymbol{F}^{H}$, where $\boldsymbol{F}^{H}$ is the conjugate transpose. It follows from $\boldsymbol{F}^{H}\boldsymbol{F}=N\boldsymbol{I}$. The rows of $\boldsymbol{F}$ are orthogonal and each has squared length $N$, which makes $\boldsymbol{F}/\sqrt N$ unitary (a unitary matrix is a square complex matrix whose conjugate transpose equals its inverse). Then $\lVert\boldsymbol{F}\mathbf{x}\rVert=\sqrt N\,\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert$ (Parseval), every singular value of $\boldsymbol{F}$ equals $\sqrt N$, and the condition number is 1. What it shows: the transform loses nothing (the 128 coefficients can rebuild the 128 samples), and it does not amplify rounding error or noise, so it is a safe change of basis.
51. **Why are the bins 50 Hz apart?** It is not chosen directly: bin spacing is the sample rate divided by the number of samples, $f_s/N=6400/128=50\,\mathrm{Hz}$. It also equals 1 divided by the frame length (20 ms), which is the finest detail a 20 ms frame can show. We picked 128 samples because it is a power of two, which our FFT needs, and it keeps each frame short, so the LED updates quickly. 50 Hz is fine enough to put the bass range (50–250 Hz) into 5 bins. Finer bins would need a longer frame: 256 samples (about 40 ms) would give 25 Hz bins, but the LED would react more slowly.
