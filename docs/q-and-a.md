# Fourier demo Q&A bank

1. **What is the Fourier transform?** It represents a signal using sinusoidal frequency components instead of values over time.
2. **Why show both the continuous transform and DFT?** The continuous form introduces the idea; the ESP32 records a finite sample vector, so its actual computation is the DFT.
3. **What is the FFT?** It is an algorithm that computes the same DFT coefficients with structured reuse. It is not a different transform.
4. **Where is the linear algebra?** For $N$ samples, the DFT is $\mathbf{X}=F\mathbf{x}$, where $F$ is an $N\times N$ complex Fourier matrix and both $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{X}$ are $N\times 1$ vectors.
5. **What does one Fourier-matrix row do?** Its complex inner product with the input measures the coefficient for one frequency-bin direction.
6. **Why are complex numbers needed?** Their real and imaginary parts encode amplitude and phase relative to cosine and sine components.
7. **Why do real signals produce paired coefficients?** They have conjugate symmetry: the upper-half coefficient is the negative-frequency partner of a lower-half coefficient.
8. **What sign and normalization do you use?** The forward exponent is negative and the forward transform is unnormalized. The inverse therefore includes $1/N$.
9. **Is the entire sound-to-colour system linear?** No. The DFT is linear, but magnitude, thresholding, clipping, and RGB mapping introduce nonlinear steps.
10. **How did you verify correctness?** Known inputs have expected coefficients, DFT and FFT full complex outputs agree at four sizes, and a numerical superposition test verifies linearity.
11. **Why is the live frame 128 samples?** It is a power of two for radix-2 FFT and gives a short 20 ms nominal frame at 6400 samples per second.
12. **Why are bins 50 Hz apart?** Bin spacing is $f_s/N = 6400/128 = 50\,\mathrm{Hz}$.
13. **Does 50 Hz bin spacing mean any two tones 50 Hz apart are always resolved?** No. Resolution also depends on frame length, windowing, signal strength, noise, and leakage.
14. **What is the Nyquist frequency?** Half the sample rate, 3200 Hz here. Frequencies above it can alias into lower apparent frequencies.
15. **Does the project have a verified anti-alias filter?** No verified external filter is claimed, so controlled tones stay below Nyquist and music results are qualitative.
16. **Why subtract the frame mean?** It removes the microphone's DC offset so the zero-frequency coefficient does not dominate.
17. **Why apply a Hamming window?** It reduces leakage from discontinuities at the frame edges, while broadening peaks and changing magnitude scaling.
18. **What happens at 225 Hz?** It lies between 200 and 250 Hz bin centres, so its energy spreads across nearby coefficients rather than occupying one bin.
19. **Why should DFT be $O(N^2)$?** It calculates $N$ output rows, each using $N$ input terms.
20. **Why should FFT be $O(N\log N)$?** Radix-2 recursion has $\log_2 N$ stages with about $N$ work per stage.
21. **Does the timing table prove asymptotic complexity?** No. It measures these implementations at finite sizes and supports the structural explanation.
22. **What exactly is timed?** Only the transform call. Acquisition, preprocessing, output magnitude, serial printing, LED updates, and test-signal construction are outside the timed region. The current FFT call includes its internal copy and twiddle calculations.
23. **How is measurement noise handled?** Runs are warmed up and repeated; the median, minimum, and maximum are reported, and a checksum consumes outputs.
24. **How are frequencies mapped to colour?** Bins 1–5 contribute red, 6–20 green, and 21–50 blue. DC and higher bins do not contribute.
25. **Why use fixed gains?** Fixed calibration preserves comparisons. Independent per-band normalization could make a weak component look falsely equal to a strong one.
26. **Why might equal generated tones not look yellow?** The speaker, room, microphone, electronics, and LED channels have different responses, so physical calibration is required.
27. **Can the LED reconstruct the sound?** No. Three brightness values summarize selected band strengths; most coefficient amplitude and all phase detail are discarded.
28. **What if the live demonstration fails?** The team can use labelled saved device output and the synthetic benchmark while still explaining the complete mathematics and limitation.
29. **Why does the Fourier matrix have 128 rows?** The frame holds $N=128$ samples, and a DFT of $N$ samples gives $N$ coefficients, so $F_{128}$ is $128\times128$. The ADC sets the sample rate (6400 Hz); the frame length sets the row count. Together they set the row spacing, $6400/128 = 50\,\mathrm{Hz}$.
30. **Why are only rows 0–64 used?** For real input, row $128-k$ is the complex conjugate of row $k$: $F_{128-k,n}=e^{-i2\pi n}\,e^{+i2\pi kn/128}=\overline{F_{k,n}}$, because $e^{-i2\pi n}=1$ for whole-number $n$. So $X_{128-k}=\overline{X_{k}}$ and $|X_{128-k}|=|X_{k}|$. Rows 65–127 repeat rows 63–1 and add no information. Rows 0 (DC) and 64 (Nyquist, 3200 Hz) are their own partners, which leaves $1+63+1=65$ distinct frequencies.
31. **Why is anything mirrored at all?** A real sine is two rotations, $\cos\theta=\tfrac12(e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta})$, so a 200 Hz tone has energy at $+200$ Hz and $-200$ Hz. At 6400 samples per second, $-200$ Hz produces exactly the same samples as $6400-200=6200$ Hz, which is row 124. Nothing is mirrored by choice; it follows from the input being real.
32. **Why does row 0 not drive a colour?** Row 0 is the frame's average level, not sound. The microphone sits at a DC bias near mid-scale, and the firmware subtracts the frame mean before the FFT anyway. Counting row 0 as bass would make the LED react to bias rather than music.
33. **Why does the LED stop at row 50 (2500 Hz) instead of row 64 (3200 Hz)?** The cutoff is set by `TREBLE_LAST_BIN = 50` in `LightingConfig.h`; the code does not record a reason. The likely reasons: the ESP32 ADC has no anti-alias filter, so content above 3200 Hz folds into the top rows (a 3500 Hz sound appears at 2900 Hz, row 58); the Hamming window spreads energy into neighbouring rows, so rows near 64 collect spill and folded energy; and small speakers and microphones respond weakly up there, so those rows are mostly noise that would make blue flicker. A safe answer: "We stop at 2500 Hz to keep a margin below the 3200 Hz Nyquist limit, because the ESP32 ADC has no anti-aliasing filter." If that is the real reason, record it next to `TREBLE_LAST_BIN`.
34. **What does the leakage step do?** It finds the strongest weighted channel, sets a floor $t=0.15\max(\mathbf{u})$, subtracts $t$ from every channel and clamps at zero. A channel under 15% of the dominant one is treated as spill from that band and switched off. Dividing by $0.85$ returns the strongest channel to its original value, so only weak channels change.
35. **Where does 0.12 come from?** It is a hand-tuned brightness scale: $0.12 = 255/2125$, so a cleaned band strength of 2125 drives a channel fully on (PWM 255). It was set for the demo speaker volume and must be retuned after hardware, volume, or speaker-position changes. As a rough guide, with the Hamming window a pure tone of amplitude $A$ ADC counts gives $|X_{k}|\approx 0.54\cdot\tfrac{128}{2}A\approx 34.6A$, and the two neighbouring rows add about $14.7A$ each, so the band strength is about $40A$. Full brightness therefore corresponds to a tone of roughly 50–60 ADC counts, under 3% of the 12-bit range, before the channel gains are applied.
36. **Why must $F$ be square? Could it be tall?** A tall $M\times N$ matrix ($M>N$) with full column rank is still injective, so $\mathbf{x}$ can be recovered with a left inverse and no information is lost. It has no two-sided inverse, though, and its $M$ outputs are redundant: they are coordinates in an overcomplete set (a frame), not in a basis. We use a square $N\times N$ matrix so $N$ samples map to exactly $N$ coefficients, a genuine change of basis. Tall versions exist in practice: a zero-padded (oversampled) DFT samples the spectrum more finely without adding information.

37. **Why is $F_N$ dense but its factors sparse?** $F_N$ has no zero entries, but its four blocks are built from the same half-size matrix $F_{N/2}$ (after the even columns are moved first): the top-left and bottom-left blocks are both $F_{N/2}$, and the right blocks are $DF_{N/2}$ and $-DF_{N/2}$. Pulling the repeated $F_{N/2}$ out leaves $B_N=\begin{bmatrix}I&D\\I&-D\end{bmatrix}$, which holds only identity and diagonal blocks, so each row has two non-zero entries. Repeating on $F_{N/2}$ gives $F_N=A_m\cdots A_2A_1P$.
38. **Is the FFT an approximation of the DFT?** No. It is an exact factorisation of the same matrix, $F_N=A_m\cdots A_1P$, so the two routes differ only by floating-point rounding. Checked on the ESP32's own frame (128 samples), the largest difference between the two spectra is below $10^{-14}$ in 64-bit floats.
39. **How many butterflies and how many non-zero entries?** Each stage has $N/2=64$ butterflies, and there are $\log_2 128=7$ stages, so $448$ butterflies. A butterfly uses 4 non-zero entries (two 1s and $\pm W$), so $448$ butterflies hold $1792$ non-zero entries, which is $7$ factors of $256$, against $128^2=16384$ in $F_{128}$.
40. **Why does one split save about half the dense work?** A dense $N\times N$ matrix costs $N^2$ products. After the split there are two dense $\tfrac N2\times\tfrac N2$ matrices, $2(\tfrac N2)^2=N^2/2$, plus the sparse $B_N$ with $2N$ non-zero entries. Repeating this $\log_2N$ times gives about $2N\log_2N$ in total.
41. **Is the permutation $P$ built as a matrix in the code?** No. $P$ has a single 1 in each row and column, with row $i$ having its 1 in column $\mathrm{rev}(i)$, the index with its bits reversed. Multiplying by it only reorders the samples, so the firmware swaps $x_i$ with $x_{\mathrm{rev}(i)}$ whenever $i<\mathrm{rev}(i)$. For $N=128$ that is 56 swaps.
42. **Why is the reordering a bit reversal?** Each even/odd split reads one bit of the index: bit $b_0$ decides which half, $b_1$ which quarter, and so on. After all the splits the first bit read becomes the top bit of the new position, so position $=\mathrm{rev}(n)=(b_0b_1\cdots b_{m-1})_2$.
43. **Why does $N$ have to be a power of two?** The radix-2 split halves the problem evenly $\log_2N$ times, down to single samples. Other lengths need a mixed-radix FFT or zero-padding up to the next power of two; $N=128=2^7$ is why the firmware's frame length works.
44. **Do these counts say how fast the ESP32 runs it?** No. They are operation counts. A butterfly and a product are different amounts of work, and real time also includes memory access and the loop overhead; the timing measurements (Q21–Q23) are separate.
45. **Why does $T(N)=2T(N/2)+O(N)$ give $O(N\log N)$?** Each level of the recursion has pieces whose sizes add up to $N$, so each level costs $O(N)$ (for the FFT, $N/2$ butterflies). Halving $N$ each time gives $\log_2N$ levels, so the total is $O(N)$ per level times $\log_2N$ levels, which is $O(N\log N)$. For $N=128$ that is 7 levels of 64 butterflies, 448 in all.

46. **Is bit shifting what makes the FFT fast?** No. The saving from $O(N^2)$ to $O(N\log N)$ comes from reusing the half-size blocks. Bit operations make the bookkeeping cheap: one even/odd split is a rotate right of the index bits, and the full reordering is the permutation matrix $P$. As a dense matrix product $P\mathbf{x}$ would cost $N^2=16384$ multiply-adds for $N=128$; the firmware does the same job with 56 swaps and no multiplications, using `>>`, `&` and `^`.
47. **Why must $N$ be a power of two?** The split halves the problem evenly down to single samples, so $N=2^m$ and every index is an $m$-bit number: each split reads one bit. The firmware checks this with `n & (n - 1) == 0` and rejects other sizes. Other lengths need a mixed-radix FFT or zero-padding to the next power of two.
48. **How is $\Pi_N$ related to the index bits?** $\Pi_N$ puts the even samples first, then the odd samples. The sample at index $n=(b_{m-1}\cdots b_1b_0)_2$ moves to position $(b_0b_{m-1}\cdots b_1)_2$, a rotate right of the index by one bit. The next level rotates the lower $m-1$ bits inside each half, and the product of all the levels is the bit-reversal matrix $P$, with $P_{ij}=1$ exactly when $j=\mathrm{rev}(i)$.
49. **How many row exchanges does $P$ need?** The rows that stay put are the indices whose bit string reads the same backwards, $2^{\lceil m/2\rceil}$ of them, and the rest swap in pairs: $(N-2^{\lceil m/2\rceil})/2$ exchanges. For $N=128$ that is $(128-16)/2=56$.
