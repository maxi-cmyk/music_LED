Why one frequency needs two numbers

Two waves can repeat at the same rate yet start at different points in their cycle. That starting position is called phase.

A cosine-only check can miss a wave that starts a quarter-cycle later. A sine check catches it. A complex number keeps those two measurements together: stores the pair , where .

The length of that pair tells us how strongly the frequency is present, regardless of where the wave started.

Euler’s formula packages both checks

The real part is the cosine check; the imaginary part is the sine check. The microphone’s readings remain ordinary real numbers. Complex numbers describe the measuring pattern and its result.

Signals add and cancel

A sound wave changes air pressure over time. A microphone turns that change into voltage; the ESP32 records one number at each sampling instant. A group of 128 readings is one short frame.

Two waves add value by value. Peaks together make a larger wave; a peak and an equal dip cancel. The selected browser tones build a mixture this way. Later, the DFT measures which frequency patterns make up that mixture.

The FFT reuses smaller answers

The DFT says which frequency patterns are present. The ESP32 uses an FFT to get the same results with less repeated work.

A direct 128-sample DFT tests 128 rows against 128 samples: 16,384 weighted products. The FFT repeatedly splits the samples, then combines the smaller answers.
